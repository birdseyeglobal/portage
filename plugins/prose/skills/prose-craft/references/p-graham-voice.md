## I. Voice

Write like you're talking to a smart friend. Not lecturing. Not performing.
Just saying what you think.

### Person

Use "I." Own it. "I think," "I've noticed," "at YC we tell founders." You're
the one saying it.

Address the reader as "you." Not "one" or "the reader." You.

Use "we" carefully. Graham often uses it as bait — the "we" of *What You Can't
Say* gets dismantled by the essay itself. Default to "I" and "you." Reach for
"we" only when you're examining the group, not hiding inside it.

### Register range

The voice has range. It's not always calm.

Against people, it softens. Against institutions, it sharpens. Graham describes
school as "six years memorizing meaningless facts in a world ruled by a caste of
giants who run after an oblong brown ball." He wouldn't write that about a
person.

Stated contempt is fine when the target is a structure. Performed outrage is
never fine. The difference: would you say it out loud?

### Honesty and hedging

Hedge precisely, not habitually. "I think," "it seems," "probably" — these are
calibration, not weakness. Use them where you're genuinely uncertain.

Hedge in the footnotes; assert in the body. The main text earns its force by
offloading qualifications below. *Mean People Fail* hedges aggressively in
footnotes but stays declarative in the body.

If you believe something, say it. "The conventional wisdom here is wrong" is a
sentence Graham will write. Admit when you don't know. It's more credible than
pretending.

## II. Sentences and Punctuation

### Sentence construction

Short. Then shorter.

Fragments land points. One idea. Full stop.

Mix short with long, but lean short. Long sentences explain mechanisms or build
sequences. They're never there to sound sophisticated.

If a sentence needs three commas, break it up. Never nest clauses inside clauses.

Parallelism compresses arguments. "Startups don't win by attacking. They win by
transcending." Two short parallel sentences do the work of a paragraph.

### Words

Use ordinary words. "Use" not "utilize." "Start" not "initiate."

Concrete nouns over abstract ones. "Stripe's founders would set up your account
on your laptop, right then" beats "a high-touch customer acquisition strategy."

Active verbs. Passive voice hides the actor.

Abstract nouns can be subjects — when the essay's job is to carve the
distinction between them. "Persistent" and "obstinate" appear as subjects dozens
of times in *The Right Kind of Stubborn* because distinguishing them is the
essay. The ban applies when the abstraction is scenery, not when it's the topic.

### Three punctuation tools

**Em-dashes** insert personal asides without splitting the sentence. "...saving
up bugs — I like debugging." The dash means: I'm telling you about me for a
second. Use it when a confession belongs inside the claim.

**Parentheticals** flag dubious assumptions or handle objections without
derailing the sentence. "(assuming, as everyone seemed to then, that
intelligence is a scalar)" — that's a bookmark. *I notice this is shaky;
moving on.*

**Footnotes** are a pressure-release valve. Hedges, qualifications, political
asides, self-corrections — everything that would muddy the main text. *How to Do
Great Work* has 29. *Keep Your Identity Small* puts its best line — the
cupboard-sign metaphor — in a footnote. Don't treat footnotes as a citation
dump. Some of your best sentences belong there.

## III. Paragraphs

Short. Two to four sentences. One idea, developed.

Paragraph length is a tool. Three sizes, three jobs:

- **One-sentence paragraph**: aphorism or hinge. "If you're interested, you're
  not astray." Makes the reader stop.
- **Two-word paragraph**: refusal. "I don't." In *Cities and Ambition*, that's a
  full paragraph. The brevity is the argument.
- **One-word paragraph**: "Why?" Pivots from observation to explanation.

These aren't tics. Each shape does a different rhetorical job.

No topic-sentence-evidence-conclusion formula. That's high school. A paragraph is
a thought — complete enough to stand alone, connected to what's around it.

## IV. Structure

Don't plan the structure before you know what you think. Write to find out.

### Three essay modes

Pick the mode before drafting.

**Thesis-first.** State the distinction, then explain it. *Maker's Schedule*
opens with both schedules already named. Best for claims you can state in a
sentence.

**Category-first.** Propose a new category, then live inside it. *Hackers and
Painters* asserts that hackers and painters are both "makers." Once the reader
accepts the category, every later claim follows. Best for reframing.

**Investigation-first.** Pose a question. Iterate through hypotheses. Knock each
down until you land. *Why Nerds Are Unpopular* proposes several explanations
before settling. The reader watches you think. Best when the path to the answer
matters as much as the answer.

### Decompose early, exhaust fully

Graham often builds an essay around a small numbered decomposition. "Two
fundamental causes." "Five qualities." "Four steps." Name the number, then walk
each one.

The decomposition is load-bearing. It's how short essays feel like full
treatments. Announce the number before enumerating, so the reader has a shape to
hold. Then actually exhaust the list. No trailing "and others."

Name three and deliver two, and you've broken the frame.

### Named sections

Most Graham essays are continuous prose. Long ones with distinct phases use
labeled sections — but labels preview a mood, not a topic. *The Conformist
Test*, *Heresy*, *Prigs*. Not *Background*, *Analysis*, *Conclusion*. If the
label sounds like a business-school chapter heading, cut it.

## V. Openings

The opening commits. It does one of these jobs: makes a claim the reader wants
to test, invokes a feeling or memory, creates a puzzle, or says something most
people haven't said out loud. What it never does: explain what you're about to
say.

### Patterns

**Bold prediction.** State what will happen. No hedging. "In a couple decades
there won't be many people who can write." (*Writes and Write-Nots*)

**Counterintuitive inversion.** Flip the obvious advice. "The way to get
startup ideas is not to try to think of startup ideas." (*How to Get Startup
Ideas*). The inversion has to be true, not just contrarian.

**Simple personal statement.** Your own practice. Plainly stated. "I try to
write using ordinary words and simple sentences." (*Write Simply*)

**Surprising link.** Connect two things that seem unrelated. State the
connection like it's obvious. "Writing that sounds good is more likely to be
right." (*Good Writing*)

**Named misconception.** State what people wrongly believe. "One of the most
common types of advice we give at Y Combinator is to do things that don't
scale." (*Do Things that Don't Scale*)

**Specific scene.** Start with something that happened. "When we were in junior
high school, my friend Rich and I made a map of the school lunch tables
according to popularity." (*Why Nerds Are Unpopular*)

**Shared experience.** Put the reader in a memory they already have. "Remember
the essays you had to write in high school?" (*The Age of the Essay*)

**Philosophical question, resolved personally.** Open with a question that
sounds unanswerable, then answer it through experience. *Life is Short* asks
whether life really is short, then answers through becoming a parent.

**Redefine the thing.** Question what the subject actually is. *How to Write
Usefully* opens by asking what essays should accomplish. Not persuade — be
useful. That reframing is the whole essay.

**The metaphor upfront.** Lead with a concrete image from another domain. Let
the reader sit with it before you explain what it illuminates. *The Bus Ticket
Theory of Genius* opens with bus ticket collectors before connecting to genius.

**Methodological announcement.** State how the essay was built. "I decided to
find out by making it." (*How to Do Great Work*). Reserve for ambitious,
synthetic essays — the method frame looks pretentious in short pieces.

**Recent realization.** Stage it as a dated insight. "I finally realized today
why politics and religion yield such uniquely useless discussions." (*Keep Your
Identity Small*). The temporal marker signals you saw something new.

**Diagnosis.** Start with a common complaint. Reveal it as a symptom. "One
reason programmers dislike meetings so much is that they're on a different type
of schedule from other people." (*Maker's Schedule*)

**Clear-the-board.** State a fact about yourself. Name the two obvious readings.
Reject both. "When I finished grad school in computer science I went to art
school to study painting. [...] Both of these images are wrong." (*Hackers and
Painters*)

**Cliche refined.** Endorse the conventional wisdom. Then narrow it. "Successful
people tend to be persistent." (*The Right Kind of Stubborn*). The truism is
true but coarse — the essay gives it resolution.

### Fusing patterns

These aren't exclusive categories. Graham fuses them routinely. *Maker's
Schedule* fuses diagnosis with surprising link. *Keep Your Identity Small* fuses
named misconception with recent realization. If two fit, let them fuse.

## VI. Argument Moves

### Analogies

**Diagnostic analogy.** An analogy shouldn't be pretty. It should reveal
structure. Same vehicle, different broken part. "The persistent are like boats
whose engines can't be throttled back. The obstinate are like boats whose
rudders can't be turned." The reader can see the difference, not just follow it.

**Domain-borrowed analogy.** Import a structure from a field the reader already
knows. "Having a meeting is like throwing an exception." You're assuming
expertise, not performing it. Don't reach into a domain the reader isn't in.

**Single deep analogy.** One comparison, carried across the whole essay, cashed
in for sub-claims. *Hackers and Painters* runs painter/hacker for thousands of
words. One deep beats three shallow.

**Personification as architecture.** Give the things you're comparing a voice.
"New York tells you, above all: you should make more money." Each instance
speaks in its own register. (*Cities and Ambition*)

### Concessions

**Concession-as-weapon.** Concede the strongest objection in a short clause.
Use the concession to narrow where your claim runs. "Of course, it's safe to
have beliefs that you treat as unquestionable if and only if they're also
unfalsifiable." The "of course" frames your next claim as the careful version,
not the naive one.

**Refused softening.** Grant that the thing you're arguing against has some use.
Refuse to let it weaken the thesis. "The optimal amount of obstinacy is not
zero." Just large enough to be honest. Not one word larger.

**Rival explanation, raised and killed.** Surface the obvious alternative. Knock
it down with one specific counterexample. "One possible explanation is that they
deal with questions that have no definite answers... But this isn't true." Raise
the rival yourself — it's cheaper than waiting for the reader to think of it.

### Reframing

**Categorical reassignment.** Propose a new category by assertion. Don't argue
it — install it and live inside it. "What hackers and painters have in common is
that they're both makers." Once the reader accepts, every later claim is a
consequence.

**Invert the assumption.** Flip the conventional framing. Not "people with X are
broken" but "there's something wrong with people who don't have X." "Almost
certainly, there is something wrong with you if you don't think things you don't
dare say out loud." Use carefully — the new assignment has to be defensible, not
just provocative.

**The step-beyond.** Name the standard position. Then name a harder one. "There
is a step beyond thinking of yourself as x but tolerating y: not even to
consider yourself an x." The reader feels their position was a waystation, not a
destination.

### Specificity and evidence

**Named, not categorical.** Stripe, not "a high-touch company." The Collisons,
not "certain founders." Leonardo's Ginevra de Benci, not "great painters."
Specificity is a commitment device — once you've named the example, you can't
handwave around it.

**Autobiographical test.** Don't just assert the thesis. Show you tested it on
yourself. "I tried living in Florence when I was 25, thinking it would be an art
center." "I tried X and it didn't work" is more persuasive than any argument.

**Self-surprise as signal.** Voice astonishment at your own claim. "Even after
decades of thinking about this, I find that sentence startling." It flags the
sentence as worth stopping at. Use rarely — the move only works because Graham
does it rarely.

### Compression

**Parallel sentences.** "Dressing oddly gets you laughed at. Violating moral
fashions can get you fired, ostracized, imprisoned, or even killed." The
parallel structure holds two things side by side; the difference between them is
the argument.

**Aphorism-as-rule.** "Argue with idiots, and you become an idiot." Compress a
claim into a proverb. Don't manufacture them — aphorisms written to sound
aphoristic read as cheap.

## VII. Closings

Graham doesn't recap. He doesn't summarize what he just said. He ends when the
idea is finished.

The closer picks the frame. Each pattern commits to a relationship with the
reader.

**Aphoristic crystallization.** Compress the whole essay into one sentence shaped
like a proverb. "The more labels you have for yourself, the dumber they make
you." (*Keep Your Identity Small*). Read it without the essay. If it still says
something, you've got a crystallization.

**Metaphor-turn.** Close with a fresh image that hasn't appeared before. "If you
stick close enough to an interesting enough question, it may grow into a
mountain beneath you." (*Superlinear Returns*). One picture, arriving once, at
the end.

**Commission.** Hand the reader something to do. "Always be questioning. That's
the only defence." (*What You Can't Say*). Works when the thesis is a
disposition, not a fact.

**Permission.** Lower the stakes. "There's nothing wrong with that." (*Cities
and Ambition*). Releases pressure after a prescriptive essay.

**Objection deflated.** Surface the reader's strongest objection. Grant it.
Shrug. "Yes, you'll have to work hard. But again, lots of people have to work
hard." (*How to Do Great Work*). Pre-empts dismissal by owning it.

**Applied idea.** Don't restate the thesis — use it. Apply it to a household
policy, a count, a personal habit. "Being mean makes you fail." (*Mean People
Fail*). Application is always more convincing than summary.

**Stance shift.** Change grammatical person or register in the final sentence.
"All we ask from those on the manager's schedule is that they understand the
cost." (*Maker's Schedule*). The essay ends as a different kind of document.

**The dare.** End with a challenge. Not rhetorical — real. "The discoveries are
out there, waiting to be made. Why not by you?" (*How to Do Great Work*). Only
works when the essay has given the reader enough to act on.

## VIII. The Tests

**Read it aloud.** If it sounds like a person talking, keep it. If it sounds
like a committee wrote it, cut it.

**The one-sentence test.** What is this piece saying? If you can't answer in one
sentence, the piece doesn't know what it thinks yet.

**The move test.** Delete any analogy, concession, or example. If the argument
survives, the move wasn't doing work.

**The footnote test.** Can the main text stand alone without the footnotes? If
yes, you've used footnotes correctly — as a pressure-release valve. If no,
you've buried load-bearing content.

**The match test.** Does the closer match the opener? Not in content — in voice.
The closer should feel like the same person who wrote the opener, but further
along.

## What to Avoid

- Throat-clearing ("In this essay I will argue...")
- Summarizing at the end
- Passive voice as default
- Abstract nouns as subjects — unless the essay is about them
- Signposting ("Firstly... Secondly... In conclusion...")
- Fancy words where plain ones work
- Jargon without definition
- Hedging every claim until nothing is said
- Pretty analogies that don't reveal structure
- Decompositions that don't close
- Starting paragraphs with "And" more than once per essay
